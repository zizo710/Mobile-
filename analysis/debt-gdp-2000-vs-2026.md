# US Debt as a Percent of GDP: 2000 → 2026

*Companion to [`us-market-2026-vs-1998-2002.md`](us-market-2026-vs-1998-2002.md).
Written 30 July 2026. Current-year figures are CBO/PGPF; historical figures
are standard OMB/Treasury/Federal Reserve public record.*

---

## TL;DR

The federal balance sheet is the single biggest macro difference between the
two episodes — and it runs entirely the wrong way for the analog's comfort.

In FY2000 the US ran a **surplus of 2.4% of GDP** (the largest since 1948),
debt held by the public was **under 35% of GDP and falling**, and the
Treasury's actual problem was a *shortage of bonds* — it bought back $30B of
long bonds in calendar 2000 because surpluses were retiring the market. In
FY2026 the deficit is **5.8% of GDP at full employment**, debt held by the
public is **~101% of GDP** (gross ~123%), and **net interest costs $1.0
trillion — 3.3% of GDP, a record share, larger than the defense budget** and
projected by CBO to reach $2.1T (4.6% of GDP) by 2036.

Every prior bust in this story was absorbed by the sovereign balance sheet.
The next one arrives with the absorber already at its historical capacity.

---

## 1. The trajectory

Debt held by the public, % of GDP (fiscal years; gross debt in parentheses):

| Year | Public debt / GDP | Deficit(−)/Surplus(+) | Net interest / GDP | What was happening |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2000 | **~34% (56%)** | **+2.4%** | ~2.3% | Third consecutive surplus; Treasury buybacks; CBO projected debt *eliminated* by ~2009 |
| 2003 | ~35% (60%) | −3.4% | ~1.4% | Dot-com bust + recession + tax cuts + wars: the full countercyclical swing cost only ~2pts of debt |
| 2007 | ~35% (62%) | −1.1% | ~1.7% | Pre-GFC plateau |
| 2009 | ~52% (83%) | −9.8% | ~1.3% | GFC absorption begins |
| 2012 | ~70% (99%) | −6.7% | ~1.4% | Household deleveraging socialized: +35pts in five years |
| 2019 | ~79% (106%) | −4.6% | ~1.8% | No consolidation at full employment — the structural break |
| 2020 | ~100% (126%) | −14.7% | ~1.6% | COVID: +21pts in a single year |
| 2023 | ~97% (120%) | −6.2% | ~2.4% | Inflation shrank the ratio; rates began repricing the stock |
| **2026** | **~101% (123%)** | **−5.8%** | **3.3% ($1.0T)** | Full employment, CPI 3.5–4.2%, hiking bias — and a war-sized deficit |
| 2036 (CBO) | rising past the 1946 record (~106%) around the turn of the decade | ~−6%+ | 4.6% ($2.1T) | Interest becomes the largest driver of the deficit itself |

Three numbers tell the whole story: **34 → 101 → 3.3.** Debt tripled as a
share of the economy, and the carrying cost — suppressed for two decades by
falling rates — has now overtaken the pre-2000 record *while* rates are still
being debated upward.

### The private-sector mirror

The composition matters as much as the level. Since 2000 the leverage has
*migrated*, not merely grown:

- **Households:** ~70% of GDP (2000) → ~98% peak (2008) → back to ~70%
  today. The household balance sheet is the one genuinely repaired ledger.
- **Nonfinancial corporates:** ~45% (2000) → ~47–49% today — moderate in
  aggregate, but this is precisely where the AI capex debt wave (~$1.5T of
  projected issuance; hyperscaler capex now ~32% debt-funded, per doc #1) is
  being written as of 2026.
- **Federal government:** ~34% → ~101%. The sovereign absorbed each private
  deleveraging in turn.

The pattern across the three busts of this quarter-century is mechanical:
the 2000–02 corporate/telecom bust cost the public balance sheet ~2 points;
2008–12 (households) cost ~35; 2020 cost ~21 in a year. Each rescue was
bigger than the last, and each was financed at progressively *lower* rates —
a luxury that has now expired.

## 2. What 2000's balance sheet bought — the part of the analog that cannot repeat

The 1998–2002 template's *ending* depended on policy space that no longer
exists in anything like the same size:

| Policy lever | 2000–03 actual | 2026 equivalent available |
|---|---|---|
| Fed funds | 6.50% → 1.00% (**550bp of cuts**) | 3.50–3.75% ceiling, with a *hiking* bias and CPI at 3.5–4.2% — perhaps 250–300bp of cuts, and only if inflation permits |
| Fiscal swing | +2.4% surplus → −3.4% deficit (**~6pts of GDP of stimulus**) from a 34% debt base | Already at −5.8% before any downturn, from a 101% base; a GFC-half-sized response implies deficits >10% and debt ~115–125% |
| Long bond | 10yr 6.8% → ~3.1%; **Treasuries were the hedge that worked** — a 60/40 portfolio was rescued by its bond side through the entire equity bust | 10yr at 4.66% and at 2026 *highs* on oil and supply; the bond rally in a downturn must fight $1T of interest issuance, term-premium concerns, and an inflation floor |
| Bond-market posture | Treasury *retiring* debt; scarcity premium in long bonds | Treasury issuing at record peacetime scale into a market already asking who the marginal buyer is |

This reframes the scenario odds from doc #1 in an important way. The
probability of the *initial* bust isn't much changed by the fiscal picture —
bubbles pop on their own internal logic. What changes is the **distribution
of outcomes after the break**:

1. **The rescue is slower and smaller.** In 2001 the Fed cut 100bp within
   three months of the top with CPI benign. A 2027 Fed facing 3.5% CPI and
   an oil shock cannot move at that speed without abandoning the target.
   Drawdowns run longer before the put strikes.
2. **The "clean escape" scenario quietly assumed fiscal room.** Part of any
   soft-landing path is the state absorbing private stress (chip subsidies,
   power buildout, backstops for systemically large AI credit). At 101%
   debt/GDP and $1T of interest, each of those is a harder vote.
3. **A new tail exists that 2000 did not have: fiscal dominance.** In every
   prior episode the bond market *cooperated* with the rescue — yields fell
   when equities broke. The 2026 version risks the opposite correlation: a
   bust that forces 10%+ deficits into a Treasury market already at record
   supply could push long yields *up* during the equity decline (the 2022
   pattern, at larger scale). That is the scenario in which the 60/40
   portfolio fails on both sides — and it has no 1998–2002 precedent.

## 3. Implications for the analysis and the books

- **It strengthens the credit-transmission thesis** (docs #1–2): the AI
  capex debt wave is being issued into a capital market where the sovereign
  is already the largest borrower at record scale. Crowding-out is not
  theoretical at $1T of federal interest — private AI credit will be priced
  against an ever-growing risk-free supply, which is exactly the mechanism
  that widens spreads *before* equities notice.
- **It weakens the traditional hedge.** The 2000-vintage playbook — long
  Treasuries as the bust hedge — worked because debt was 34% of GDP and
  falling. At 101% and rising, the defensive leg of the barbell argues for
  real assets and cheap-vol equity structures instead; this is an
  independent, macro-side justification for the XLE leg in the
  valuation-anchored book (doc #3), which was chosen on valuation grounds
  alone.
- **It caps the melt-up's policy fuel.** The 1999 blow-off ran on a Fed
  that could ease into strength (and did, into Y2K). The 2026 Fed has three
  dissents *for hiking*. If the Act-3 final leg comes, it comes on private
  liquidity — buybacks, passive flows, FOMO — not on policy accommodation,
  which historically makes it narrower and shorter.
- **Watch the long end as a signpost** (adds to doc #1's list): in the next
  serious equity drawdown, the *first thing to check* is whether the 10-year
  rallies. If Treasuries fail to hedge an equity break of 10%+, the
  fiscal-dominance tail is live, and the bust playbook is 2022's, not
  2001's.
